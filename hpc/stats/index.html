Probability and Measurement

Performance measurements vary. Interrupts arrive, addresses change, predictors learn, caches retain history, and the processor changes frequency. Probability is what lets us separate a repeatable effect from this background variation—and what lets randomized algorithms make useful promises without predicting every run.

This appendix develops the small part of probability and statistics used throughout the book. It is not a substitute for experimental design, but it should make the assumptions behind an average, an error bar, or a “with high probability” claim visible.

#Probability Spaces and Random Variables

A random experiment has a sample space $\Omega$ of possible outcomes. An event is a subset of $\Omega$, and a probability measure $P$ assigns events numbers between zero and one, with $P(\Omega)=1$ and additive probabilities for disjoint events.

A random variable is a function from outcomes to values. A die roll, for example, may have $\Omega={1,2,3,4,5,6}$ and $X(\omega)=\omega$. For a fair die,

$$ P(X=k)=\frac16. $$

An indicator $I_A$ for an event $A$ is a random variable equal to one when $A$ occurs and zero otherwise. Indicators are a simple bridge between logic and arithmetic.

For a discrete random variable, its probability mass function lists $P(X=x)$. A continuous variable instead has a density $f$ whose integral gives interval probabilities:

$$ P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx. $$

The probability of any exact real value can be zero even though some value must be observed. Densities are not probabilities at a point; they are probabilities per unit of the horizontal axis.

#Conditional Probability

When $P(B)>0$, the probability of $A$ after learning that $B$ occurred is

$$ P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}. $$

Events $A$ and $B$ are independent when $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$, equivalently when learning one does not change the probability of the other.

When $P(A)>0$ as well, writing the same intersection in the other order gives Bayes’ rule:

$$ P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}. $$

The factor $P(A)$ is the prior. Ignoring it causes the base-rate fallacy. If a rare hardware fault triggers a detector, the probability that the fault actually occurred depends not only on the detector’s accuracy but also on how rare the fault was before the alert.

#Expectation

The expected value of a discrete random variable is its probability-weighted average:

$$ \operatorname E[X]=\sum_x xP(X=x). $$

For a fair six-sided die it is $3.5$. No roll produces 3.5; expectation describes the long-run average, not a required outcome.

Expectation is linear:

$$ \operatorname E[aX+bY]=a\operatorname E[X]+b\operatorname E[Y]. $$ This identity does not require independence. That makes indicator variables unusually effective: if $$ X=\sum_i I_i, $$ then $$ \operatorname E[X]=\sum_i P(I_i=1), $$

even when the events strongly affect one another.

#Fixed Points of a Permutation

Choose a permutation of $n$ elements uniformly and let $I_i$ indicate that position $i$ contains element $i$. Each element is equally likely to occupy each position, so $P(I_i=1)=1/n$. The number of fixed points is $X=\sum_i I_i$, and

$$ \operatorname E[X] =\sum_{i=1}^n\operatorname E[I_i] =n\cdot\frac1n =1. $$

The indicators are not independent, but linearity makes that irrelevant.

#Variance and Covariance

Expectation locates a distribution. Variance measures its squared spread:

$$ \operatorname{Var}(X) =\operatorname E[(X-\operatorname E[X])^2] =\operatorname E[X^2]-\operatorname E[X]^2. $$ Its square root, the standard deviation $\sigma$, has the same units as $X$. For a constant $a$, $$ \operatorname{Var}(aX)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X). $$ The covariance $$ \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) =\operatorname E[(X-\operatorname E[X])(Y-\operatorname E[Y])] $$ controls the variance of a sum: $$ \operatorname{Var}(X+Y) =\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y) +2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y). $$

Independent variables have zero covariance, although the converse is not generally true.

For $n$ independent, identically distributed observations with variance $\sigma^2$, the sample mean

$$ \bar X=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i $$ has variance $$ \operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}. $$

Its standard deviation—the standard error of the mean—shrinks as $1/\sqrt n$. Getting one extra decimal digit from repetition alone is expensive: it requires roughly one hundred times as many independent samples.

#Computing Variance Stably

The formula $E[X^2]-E[X]^2$ can lose most of its precision when the mean is large and the variation is small. Welford’s recurrence updates centered sums without subtracting two nearly equal large numbers:

#include <cstddef>

struct RunningStats {
    std::size_t n = 0;
    long double mean = 0;
    long double m2 = 0;

    void add(long double x) {
        ++n;
        long double delta = x - mean;
        mean += delta / n;
        m2 += delta * (x - mean);
    }

    long double sample_variance() const {
        // Precondition: n >= 2.
        return m2 / (n - 1);
    }
};

The denominator $n-1$ estimates the population variance from a sample. If the collected values are the entire population of interest rather than a sample, divide by $n$ instead.

#Concentration Bounds

An expectation does not say how often a variable is close to it. Concentration inequalities turn moments or range bounds into probability statements.

#Markov and Chebyshev

For a nonnegative random variable $X$ and $a>0$, Markov’s inequality states

$$ P(X\ge a)\le\frac{\operatorname E[X]}a. $$

It follows from $aI_{X\ge a}\le X$ by taking expectations.

Applying Markov to $(X-\mu)^2$ gives Chebyshev’s inequality:

$$ P(|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma)\le\frac1{k^2}. $$

These bounds need very few assumptions and are correspondingly loose. They are useful for proofs that must hold for arbitrary distributions, not usually for tight benchmark error bars.

#Hoeffding’s Inequality

If $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ are independent and each lies in $[0,1]$, then

$$ P(|\bar X-\operatorname E[\bar X]|\ge\varepsilon) \le2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}. $$ To make this probability at most $\delta$, it is enough to take $$ n\ge\frac{\ln(2/\delta)}{2\varepsilon^2}. $$

The assumptions matter. Reusing correlated pseudo-samples or stopping an experiment as soon as the desired result appears invalidates the calculation.

#The Birthday Threshold

Hashing repeatedly asks how many random values fit into $m$ buckets before a collision becomes likely. For $n\le m$ independent uniform choices, the probability of no collision is

$$ P(\text{no collision}) =\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{m}\right). $$ When $n$ is small relative to $m$, using $\ln(1-x)\approx-x$ gives $$ P(\text{no collision}) \approx \exp\left(-\frac{n(n-1)}{2m}\right). $$

The transition therefore occurs around $n=\Theta(\sqrt m)$, not around $m$. With a 64-bit uniformly random hash, roughly $2^{32}$ values already put collisions on a constant-probability scale.

Linearity of expectation gives the expected number of colliding pairs,

$$ \operatorname E[C]=\binom n2\frac1m, $$

and Markov proves that the collision probability tends to zero when $n^2/m\to0$. Proving that it tends to one in the opposite regime needs the exact product, a second-moment argument, or another lower bound; expectation alone cannot provide both directions.

#Monte Carlo and Las Vegas Algorithms

Suppose a shape covers an unknown fraction $p$ of a unit square. Sample points uniformly and let $I_i$ indicate whether point $i$ is covered. The estimator

$$ \hat p=\frac1n\sum_i I_i $$ is unbiased: $E[\hat p]=p$. A Bernoulli variable has variance $$ p(1-p)\le\frac14, $$

so the estimator’s standard deviation is at most $1/(2\sqrt n)$. Hoeffding gives a distribution-free finite-sample guarantee: $n\ge\ln(2/\delta)/(2\varepsilon^2)$ ensures absolute error below $\varepsilon$ with probability at least $1-\delta$.

An algorithm that has a bounded running time but may return an approximate or occasionally wrong answer is usually called Monte Carlo. An algorithm that always returns a correct answer but has random running time is Las Vegas. Random-pivot quicksort is Las Vegas: pivot choices change the work, not the sorted result.

#From Samples to Error Bars

Let $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ be independent observations from a stable distribution with mean $\mu$ and finite variance. For sufficiently regular data, the central limit theorem makes the standardized sample mean approximately normal as $n$ grows. When the variance is estimated from the same sample, a common interval is

$$ \bar x\;\pm\;t_{1-\alpha/2,n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt n}, $$

where $s$ is the sample standard deviation and $t$ is a Student-$t$ quantile. Under the model assumptions, repeating the complete experiment many times would make a fraction $1-\alpha$ of such intervals cover $\mu$.

This is a confidence interval, not a probability distribution over the already-fixed parameter. It also says nothing about whether the effect is large enough to matter.

Latency distributions are often skewed or heavy-tailed. In that case, report the quantity the user experiences:

  • a mean for total throughput or expected cost;
  • a median for a typical request;
  • high quantiles for tail-latency objectives;
  • a full histogram or empirical distribution when one number conceals important modes.

Bootstrap intervals can estimate uncertainty for medians and other statistics, but resampling individual observations still assumes those observations are exchangeable. It cannot repair a confounded experiment.

#Designing Performance Experiments

The largest benchmark errors are usually systematic, not statistical. A million repetitions do not fix measuring the wrong workload. A defensible comparison should address the following points.

Choose an experimental unit. Timing every iteration inside one process does not necessarily produce independent samples; those iterations share frequency, cache, predictor, and allocator state. Often the independent unit is a complete randomized batch or a fresh process.

Pair competing implementations. Run A and B under nearby conditions with the same generated input, randomize or alternate their order, and analyze paired differences or log-ratios. Independent “all A, then all B” runs can mistake thermal drift for a speedup.

Separate warm-up from the workload. Warm-up may be appropriate for steady-state service code and wrong for startup latency. State which regime is being measured instead of automatically discarding inconvenient samples.

Predeclare the statistic and stopping rule. Repeatedly checking significance and stopping at the first favorable result inflates false-positive rates. Looking at twenty variants and reporting only the best does the same unless the comparison accounts for selection.

Report effect size and uncertainty. “Statistically significant” can describe a useless 0.1% change after enough repetitions. A speedup with an interval, workload, and machine description is more informative than a bare $p$-value.

Keep correctness in the experiment. Compare outputs against a reference over the benchmark’s actual input distribution. An implementation that silently assumes alignment, nonempty input, or absent overflow is a different program unless those conditions are part of the contract.

Good statistics cannot manufacture reproducibility, but they make uncertainty explicit. The practical rule is simple: randomize what you cannot control, pair what you can compare, preserve raw observations, and make every probability statement name its assumptions.
