Modern Hardware

The main disadvantage of the supercomputers of the 1960s wasn’t that they were slow — relatively speaking, they weren’t — but that they were giant, complex to use, and so expensive that only the governments of the world superpowers could afford them. Their size was the reason they were so expensive: they required a lot of custom components that had to be very carefully assembled in the macro-world, by people holding advanced degrees in electrical engineering, in a process that couldn’t be scaled up for mass production.

The turning point was the development of integrated circuits: complete circuits manufactured together on a small piece of silicon. The MOS Technology 6502, introduced in 1975 at a famously disruptive $25 price, placed a complete processor on a die of roughly 4 mm × 4 mm1. Falling cost and size helped start the home-computer revolution, with systems such as the Apple II, Atari 2600, Commodore 64, and IBM PC reaching a much wider market.

#How Microchips are Made

Microchips are “printed” on a slice of crystalline silicon using a process called photolithography, which involves

  1. growing and slicing a very pure silicon crystal,
  2. covering it with a layer of a substance that dissolves when photons hit it,
  3. hitting it with photons in a set pattern,
  4. chemically etching the now-exposed parts,
  5. removing the remaining photoresist,

…and repeating deposition, patterning, implantation, and interconnect steps across many layers. A modern fabrication flow contains far more operations than this sketch and can take months from wafer start to packaged chip.

Consider now the exposure step. Projection optics reduce a mask pattern onto the wafer. Resolution depends on wavelength, numerical aperture, photoresist, process control, and increasingly computational techniques such as multiple patterning—not simply on making a lens “stronger.” Even early processes could place thousands of transistors in an area smaller than a fingernail, giving integrated circuits several advantages over assemblies of discrete components:

  • higher clock rates (that were previously limited by the speed of light);
  • the ability to scale the production;
  • much lower material and power usage, translating to much lower cost per unit.

Apart from these immediate benefits, lithographic manufacturing created a repeatable path to denser circuits. Each generation still required substantial changes in devices, materials, design rules, and fabrication equipment; optical scaling was never a free source-code-compatible shrink.

#Dennard Scaling

Consider what happens when we scale a microchip down. A smaller circuit requires proportionally fewer materials, and smaller transistors take less time to switch (along with all other physical processes in the chip), allowing reducing the voltage and increasing the clock rate.

An idealized constant-field scaling argument, commonly called Dennard scaling, says that reducing transistor dimensions by 30%

  • doubles the transistor density ($0.7^2 \approx 0.5$),
  • increases the clock speed by 40% ($\frac{1}{0.7} \approx 1.4$),
  • and leaves the overall power density the same.

Under that model, each generation offers more transistors and a higher clock without increasing power density. Real manufacturing cost, yield, leakage, interconnect, packaging, and design effort complicate the picture, but the model explains why several decades of shrinking transistors also brought large automatic speedups.

Due to the trade-offs between energy and performance you can make during the design, the fidelity of the fabrication process itself, such as “180nm” or “65nm,” directly translating to the density of transistors, became the trademark for CPU efficiency2.

For much of computing history, scaling was a main driver of performance improvements. Gordon Moore predicted that economically useful component counts would grow exponentially; the commonly quoted later cadence is a doubling about every two years. The trend remained a useful industry guide for decades, although its pace, cost, and even the appropriate density metric now vary.

Both Dennard scaling and Moore’s law are not actual laws of physics, but just observations made by savvy engineers. They are both destined to stop at some point due to fundamental physical limitations, the ultimate one being the size of silicon atoms. In fact, Dennard scaling already did — due to power issues.

Nearly all electrical energy consumed by a processor eventually becomes heat. That heat must be removed through a package and cooling system, placing a practical limit on chip power density. Voltage, frequency, active units, leakage, and cooling are traded against one another; there is no single maximum clock chosen independently of the workload.

Around 2005–2007, this strategy stopped working because voltage could no longer be reduced in proportion to transistor dimensions. Lower threshold voltages make transistors switch quickly, but also increase subthreshold leakage; very thin gate oxides additionally allow electrons to tunnel through them. Interconnect resistance and delay were becoming important at the same time. The extra static and dynamic power still ends up as heat even when it performs no useful computation.

Chip designers responded with several techniques—new transistor geometries, power gating, multiple voltage and frequency domains, and more conservative clocks—but none restored the old constant-power scaling rule. At some point, clock rates could no longer be increased automatically with each process generation, even though transistor density continued to improve.

#Modern Computing

Dennard scaling has ended, but Moore’s law is not dead yet.

Clock rates plateaued, but the transistor count is still increasing, allowing for the creation of new, parallel hardware. Instead of chasing faster cycles, CPU designs started to focus on getting more useful things done in a single cycle. Instead of getting smaller, transistors have been changing shape.

This resulted in increasingly complex architectures capable of doing dozens, hundreds, or even thousands of different things every cycle.

Here are some core approaches making use of more available transistors that are driving recent computer designs:

  • Overlapping the execution of instructions so that different parts of the CPU are kept busy (pipelining);
  • Executing operations without necessarily waiting for the previous ones to complete (speculative and out-of-order execution);
  • Adding multiple execution units to process independent operations simultaneously (superscalar processors);
  • Adding vector instructions capable of applying one operation to groups packed into 128-, 256-, or 512-bit registers (SIMD);
  • Adding layers of on-chip cache to reduce the latency and traffic of accesses that would otherwise reach RAM;
  • Adding multiple identical cores on a chip (parallel computing, GPUs);
  • Using multiple chips in a motherboard and multiple cheaper computers in a data center (distributed computing);
  • Using custom hardware to solve a specific problem with better chip utilization (ASICs, FPGAs).

For modern computers, the “let’s count all operations” approach for predicting algorithm performance isn’t just slightly wrong but is off by several orders of magnitude. This calls for new computation models and other ways of assessing algorithm performance.

  2. Modern process-node names no longer identify one literal transistor dimension. They are generational labels, so comparing “nanometers” across manufacturers requires actual density, power, and performance data. ↩︎
