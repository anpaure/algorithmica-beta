Modular Arithmetic

A timestamp contains more information than most questions about time require. If it is Thursday and we want to know the weekday 365 days later, carrying the year, month, hour, and second through the calculation would be ceremony. We only need $$ (3+365)\bmod 7=4, $$

where Monday is day zero. The large counter has been projected onto a seven-position clock.

This projection is not merely a space-saving trick. It gives us an arithmetic system in which values remain bounded while addition and multiplication still behave predictably.

#Residues

Two integers $a$ and $b$ are congruent modulo a positive integer $m$ when their difference is divisible by $m$:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod m \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad m\mid(a-b). $$

Congruence partitions all integers into $m$ residue classes. We usually represent each class by its unique member in $[0,m)$, although any congruent integer denotes the same class.

The useful property is that congruence respects addition and multiplication:

$$ \begin{aligned} a\equiv b\pmod m,\;c\equiv d\pmod m &\Longrightarrow a+c\equiv b+d\pmod m,\\ &\Longrightarrow ac\equiv bd\pmod m. \end{aligned} $$

We can therefore reduce intermediate values whenever convenient. In C++, % is a remainder operator rather than a mathematical modulo operator: for a negative dividend, its result may be negative. A normalization routine must account for that:

#include <cstdint>

int64_t normalize(int64_t x, int64_t m) {
    int64_t r = x % m;
    return r < 0 ? r + m : r;
}

Even when the final residue is small, the expression evaluated before % can overflow. For 32-bit residues, use a 64-bit product; for 64-bit residues, use a wider product or a reduction algorithm such as Barrett reduction or Montgomery multiplication.

#Additive Cycles

Consider a calendar with $m$ weekdays and a year containing $a$ days. Starting at day zero, the weekday after $k$ years is

$$ d_k=ka\bmod m. $$ The cycle returns to zero at the smallest positive $k$ for which $m\mid ka$. Dividing both numbers by $g=\gcd(a,m)$ leaves two coprime integers, so the smallest such $k$ is $$ \frac{m}{\gcd(a,m)}. $$

This explains, for example, why repeatedly adding 4 modulo 10 visits only $0,4,8,2,6$: the common factor 2 confines us to five residue classes.

Multiplicative cycles are subtler. If $p$ is prime and $a$ is not divisible by $p$, the powers of $a$ are nonzero, but they do not necessarily visit all $p-1$ nonzero residues. Modulo 7, powers of 2 cycle through $1,2,4$ and return after three steps. Visiting every nonzero residue requires $a$ to be a primitive root modulo $p$.

What is guaranteed is that the cycle length divides $p-1$.

#Fermat’s Theorem

Fermat’s little theorem. For every prime $p$ and integer $a$,

$$ a^p\equiv a\pmod p. $$ If $p\nmid a$, division by $a$ in the residue system gives the equivalent form $$ a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p. $$ One compact proof uses a permutation. Multiplication by a nonzero $a$ maps the nonzero residues $$ 1,2,\ldots,p-1 $$ to $$ a,2a,\ldots,(p-1)a\pmod p. $$ No two products can be congruent: if $ai\equiv aj\pmod p$, then the prime $p$ divides $a(i-j)$; since it does not divide $a$, it must divide $i-j$. Thus the products are merely a permutation of the original residues. Multiplying every entry yields $$ a^{p-1}(p-1)!\equiv(p-1)!\pmod p. $$

None of the factors in $(p-1)!$ is divisible by $p$, so it can be canceled, leaving $a^{p-1}\equiv1$.

The theorem gives a cheap way to disprove primality. If for some $1<a<n$,

$$ a^{n-1}\not\equiv1\pmod n, $$

then $n$ is certainly composite. Passing the test is only evidence: Carmichael numbers pass it for every base coprime to them. Repeating a Fermat test does not turn that structural failure into a reliable error bound. Practical probabilistic primality testing normally uses Miller–Rabin; for fixed-width integers, a known finite set of bases makes Miller–Rabin deterministic.

#Modular Division

Addition and multiplication descend naturally to residues. Ordinary division does not. The equation

$$ bx\equiv a\pmod m $$ has a unique solution for every $a$ only when $b$ has a multiplicative inverse: a residue $b^{-1}$ satisfying $$ bb^{-1}\equiv1\pmod m. $$

Such an inverse exists exactly when $\gcd(b,m)=1$. If the gcd is larger than one, multiplication by $b$ collapses several residues together and cannot be undone. For instance, $2x\equiv1\pmod6$ has no solution, while $5^{-1}\equiv5\pmod6$ because $5\cdot5\equiv1$.

For prime $p$ and $a\not\equiv0\pmod p$, Fermat’s theorem immediately gives

$$ a^{-1}\equiv a^{p-2}\pmod p. $$

The power can be computed in $O(\log p)$ multiplications using binary exponentiation. For a composite modulus, or when primality is not known, the extended Euclidean algorithm finds the inverse under the more general condition $\gcd(a,m)=1$.

Once the inverse is known, modular division is multiplication:

$$ \frac{a}{b}\pmod m \;:=\; ab^{-1}\pmod m. $$ This notation should not hide the precondition. In modular code, “divide by $b$” is a claim that $b$ is invertible in the chosen modulus; it is worth making that contract explicit.
